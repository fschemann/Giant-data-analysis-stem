8  Scientific Communication

Guiding question: How can I communicate my findings clearly and responsibly?

In this area you will learn how to: present findings clearly, select effective visualisations, explain uncertainties and limitations, adapt communication to different audiences and create scientific reports, briefs or dashboards. (Source materials: Data Visualization: A Practical Introduction* (socviz.co), Fundamentals of Data Visualization (clauswilke.com/dataviz) — see Resources.)*

This chapter is where your work from every other competency area comes together into the two required final outputs from the overview: an interactive dashboard and a short scientific report.

8.1 Know Your Audience

The same finding needs different framing for different readers. Before building anything, decide:

  • A specialist reader (e.g. another data-science student, your instructor) wants methodological detail, model diagnostics and explicit uncertainty.
  • A policy or general audience wants the headline finding, its practical implications and honest caveats — without needing to parse a regression table.

Your scientific report is written mainly for the first audience; your dashboard is usually written for something closer to the second, even though both draw on the same underlying analysis.

8.2 Selecting Effective Visualisations

A good visualisation answers a specific question at a glance; a bad one requires a paragraph of explanation to be understood at all. A few working rules, building directly on the Grammar of Graphics from Chapter 2:

  • Match the geometry to the question. Comparing categories → bars. Showing change over time → lines. Showing a relationship between two continuous variables → scatterplot. Showing a distribution → histogram or boxplot. Don’t default to a bar chart out of habit when a line or scatterplot answers the question more directly.
  • Label everything a stranger would need. Axis titles with units, a clear legend, a data source and access date and — for any interval or model estimate — a visible indication of uncertainty (error bars, a shaded confidence band).
  • Avoid chartjunk. 3D effects, unnecessary gridlines and decorative colour gradients cost attention without adding information. Every visual element should earn its place.
  • Choose colour deliberately. Use a sequential palette for an ordered quantity, a qualitative palette for unordered categories and check your plot still reads correctly in greyscale or to a colour-blind viewer (scale_colour_viridis_d() in ggplot2 is a reliable default).

8.3 Communicating Uncertainty and Limitations Honestly

A dashboard or report that hides its uncertainty is not more convincing — it is less trustworthy once a careful reader notices what’s missing. Concretely:

  • State confidence intervals, not just point estimates, wherever you computed one in Chapter 3.
  • Name your model’s assumptions and any you couldn’t fully verify (recall the repeated-measures limitation from Exercise 4.4) — it’s better to name a limitation openly than to present a coefficient as if it came from an ideal, unlimited dataset.
  • Distinguish explicitly between what your evidence supports and what it does not establish — this is exactly the Exercise 7.4 sentence you already drafted in Chapter 7.

8.4 Building the Interactive Dashboard

Your final dashboard must be built with shinydashboard, the R package that gives a Shiny app a structured dashboard layout (header, sidebar menu and a grid of boxes) instead of a plain single-page app. This is a deliberate, required choice for this module — not one option among several — because it gives every student’s final output the same recognisable structure and because live, user-driven filtering (picking a region or year range and watching every panel update) is itself part of what this competency area is assessing.

app.R — minimal shinydashboard skeleton
library(shiny)
library(shinydashboard)
library(tidyverse)
library(plotly)

climate <- read_csv("data/clean/dwd_climate_clean.csv")

ui <- dashboardPage(
  dashboardHeader(title = "German Regional Climate — DWD, 1991-2024"),
  dashboardSidebar(
    selectInput("period", "Period:", choices = c("1991-2000", "2015-2024", "Both"), selected = "Both"),
    selectInput("region", "Region:", choices = c("All", unique(climate$Region)))
  ),
  dashboardBody(
    fluidRow(
      box(title = "Temperature over time", width = 6, plotlyOutput("temp_plot")),
      box(title = "Precipitation by region",  width = 6, plotlyOutput("precip_plot"))
    ),
    fluidRow(
      box(title = "Key finding", width = 12, textOutput("finding_text"))
    )
  )
)

server <- function(input, output) {
  # filter `climate` reactively based on input$period / input$region,
  # then render each plotlyOutput() and the finding_text from that filtered data
}

shinyApp(ui, server)

Required elements — your submitted dashboard must include all of the following, not just a working app shell:

  1. At least two linked, interactive panels (box()es) built with plotly outputs, each addressing a distinct part of your research question — not two views of the same single chart.
  2. At least one input control (selectInput(), sliderInput(), or similar) that actually changes what the panels show — a dashboard where every widget is decorative does not meet this requirement.
  3. Labels a stranger would need: axis titles with units, a legend where relevant and a visible data-source caption (dataset name and access/generation date) somewhere on the page.
  4. A visible indication of uncertainty on at least one panel where you computed one in Chapter 3 (a confidence band, error bars, or the interval stated in a caption) — a dashboard that only shows point estimates hides exactly the information §8.3 asks you to communicate honestly.
  5. One short, written “key finding” panel or caption stating, in plain language, what the dashboard’s main pattern is — the same discipline as a plot title that states a finding rather than just variable names (§8.2).
  6. A short qualitative reflection panel, alongside the key-finding panel: in a sentence or two, who is affected by this pattern, whose data or perspective might be missing from your dataset and why does this local result matter beyond this one place? This is the quantitative-and-qualitative pairing this module asks for and the same habit of mind from Chapter 7 — your dashboard should say something in words, not only in charts.
  7. Deployability. Your app.R (or ui.R/server.R) must run with shiny::runApp() from a clean checkout of your repository — see the Resources page for shinydashboard setup and deployment tutorials (including free hosting via shinyapps.io, if you want a shareable link rather than a local-only app).

8.5 Writing the Short Scientific Report

Revisit the required structure from the overview’s final-challenge section: Research Question, Environmental Context, Data, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, References and Data Sources. Write it in the order it will finally appear — this is also the order a reader experiences it in:

  1. Introduction (your Research Question) — state the problem and the specific, data-answerable question you’re addressing.
  2. A short theoretical framing (your Environmental Context) — a few sentences grounding the problem in why it matters and what is already known, not a full literature review.
  3. Methods — document your steps precisely; this doubles as your reproducibility documentation from Chapter 5.
  4. Results — the figures and numbers from Chapters 2–4.
  5. Discussion, drawing directly on your Chapter 7 evidence-based response.
  6. Conclusion.

Ask your learning buddy to read a draft section back to you and ask “what would a reader still want to know here?” rather than asking it to write the section for you outright — the Discussion and Conclusion in particular need to reflect your judgement about what the evidence does and doesn’t support.

If shinydashboard’s ui/server split feels confusing at first: RStudio’s own Shiny tutorial builds up a basic app step by step before introducing the dashboard layout on top of it — work through that first if the skeleton above doesn’t yet make sense.

If you want to go beyond the required elements — better chart design, storytelling structure, or a more polished report: Storytelling with Data (Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic) is the widely-used reference for turning correct charts into genuinely persuasive ones.

  • Can you justify, for each figure in your dashboard, why that chart type fits that question better than the alternatives?
  • Does every figure state its data source, units and (where relevant) its uncertainty?
