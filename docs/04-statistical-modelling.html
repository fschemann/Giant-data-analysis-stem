4  Statistical Modelling

Guiding question: Which factors explain the observed patterns?

In this area you will learn how to: investigate relationships between variables, use regression models, compare statistical models, evaluate model assumptions and interpret model results. (Source materials: OpenIntro IMS — Inference for Regression, r4ds — see Resources.)

library(tidyverse)
library(broom)
climate <- read_csv("data/clean/dwd_climate_clean.csv")
states <- climate %>% filter(Region != "Deutschland")
Code walkthrough
  • broom — turns base R’s often-messy model output (from lm() and others) into tidy data frames, via functions like tidy() and glance() used throughout this chapter.

Chapter 2 showed you that patterns exist (e.g. temperature seems to vary with sunshine duration). Chapter 3 showed you how to ask whether a difference is trustworthy. Statistical modelling goes one step further: it asks how much of a pattern one variable explains and lets you compare competing explanations against each other.

4.1 Simple Linear Regression

A regression model describes a variable of interest (the response, e.g. Temperature_C) as a function of one or more predictors (e.g. Sunshine_hours), plus unavoidable noise:

\[ \text{Temperature}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Sunshine}_i + \varepsilon_i \]

Think of it as fitting the straightest possible line through a scatterplot such that the total squared vertical distance between the line and every point (“residuals”) is as small as possible — the “least squares” line. You already saw the raw relationship as a scatterplot in Chapter 2; this chapter puts a number on it.

  • \(\beta_0\) (intercept): the model’s predicted response when the predictor is zero. Zero hours of annual sunshine is far outside our data’s real range, so here the intercept is a mathematical anchor, not a real-world claim.
  • \(\beta_1\) (slope): the predicted change in the response for a one-unit increase in the predictor, holding everything else constant. This is usually the number you actually care about.

Exercise 4.1 — Fit and read your first model

model_simple <- lm(Temperature_C ~ Sunshine_hours, data = states)
summary(model_simple)
tidy(model_simple)
glance(model_simple)
Code walkthrough
  • lm() — short for “linear model”; fits the straight line from the equation above. The formula reads “Temperature_C explained by Sunshine_hours”.
  • summary(model_simple) — base R’s traditional, verbose printout of the fitted model.
  • tidy(model_simple) — the same coefficients, as a tidy table (from the broom package).
  • glance(model_simple) — tidy model-level statistics (R², etc.), one row per model.

Q1. Write, in a sentence, what the estimated slope for Sunshine_hours means in climate terms (units and direction included).

Q2. What is \(R^2\) for this model? In plain language: what percentage of the variation in temperature does sunshine duration alone explain — and does that feel like a lot or a little to you?

Ask your learning buddy to explain the difference between “the slope is statistically significant” and “the slope is practically important” — this is the same effect-size lesson from Chapter 3, now applied to a model coefficient instead of a group difference.

Expected: a slope around \(+0.0015\) (temperature rises about 0.15°C for every 100 extra hours of annual sunshine) and \(R^2 \approx 0.08\) — sunshine duration alone explains only around 8% of the variation in temperature, even though the slope is statistically significant (\(p < 0.001\)) on \(n = 170\) (5 regions × 34 years). This is a real, honest example of a weak-but-real relationship: don’t expect every real predictor to explain as much as Chapter 3’s decade comparison did.

4.2 Modelling Relationships: Adding More Predictors

Real environmental patterns rarely have one cause. Multiple regression extends the same idea to several predictors at once:

\[ \text{Temperature}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Sunshine}_i + \beta_2 \cdot \text{Year}_i + \beta_3 \cdot \text{Region}_i + \varepsilon_i \]

For a categorical predictor like Region, R automatically creates indicator (“dummy”) variables against a reference category, so each coefficient reads as “the average difference compared to the reference level, holding the other predictors fixed.” Analogy: think of each dummy variable as an on/off switch — “1” if a row belongs to that region, “0” otherwise. One region (alphabetically first, by default) gets no switch of its own and becomes the reference level; its effect is baked into the model’s intercept, and every other region’s coefficient is read as “how much warmer or colder than the reference region, on average.”

Exercise 4.2 — Build a multiple regression model

model_multi <- lm(Temperature_C ~ Sunshine_hours + Year + Region, data = states)
tidy(model_multi)
glance(model_multi)

Q3. Which Region has the highest predicted temperature, holding sunshine and year constant, according to this model? Does that match what you saw visually in Chapter 2?

Q4. Compare the Sunshine_hours coefficient in model_multi to the one in model_simple (Exercise 4.1). Did it change much once year and region were added? What might explain that?

4.3 Model Comparison

Adding predictors will (almost) always increase \(R^2\) — even a useless predictor adds a tiny bit of explained variance by chance. Two tools protect you from being fooled by this:

  • Adjusted \(R^2\) penalises a model for every predictor it adds, so it only increases if a new predictor pulls more than its “fair share” of explanatory weight. Prefer adjusted \(R^2\) over plain \(R^2\) whenever you’re comparing models with a different number of predictors.
  • anova() model comparison formally tests whether a more complex model explains significantly more variance than a simpler, nested one.

Analogy: imagine judging two students’ essays not just on word count, but on how much relevant content they packed into each additional page. Plain \(R^2\) rewards length; adjusted \(R^2\) rewards density.

Exercise 4.3 — Does the extra complexity earn its place?

anova(model_simple, model_multi)

glance(model_simple)$adj.r.squared
glance(model_multi)$adj.r.squared
Code walkthrough
  • anova(model_simple, model_multi) — formally compares the two models: does the more complex one explain significantly more variance than the simpler one it’s built on top of?
  • glance(...)$adj.r.squared — glance() returns a small tidy table of model statistics; the $ pulls just one column out of it, here the adjusted R².

Q5. Did adjusted \(R^2\) increase meaningfully from model_simple to model_multi? What does the anova() p-value tell you about whether the added predictors are jointly worth their added complexity?

Q6. Chapter 3 found a real, large year-over-year warming effect. Does that help you predict, before running the code, whether Year would turn out to be a useful predictor here?

Expected: adjusted \(R^2\) jumps from about 0.07 (model_simple) to about 0.57 (model_multi) and the anova() p-value is far below 0.001. Unlike some textbook examples where extra predictors barely help, here the added complexity clearly does earn its place — Year alone captures a large, real warming trend (consistent with Chapter 3) and Region captures real, systematic differences in climate between Bavaria, Saxony and the other regions that Sunshine_hours alone could never explain.

4.4 Interpreting Models — and Checking Their Assumptions

A regression model’s coefficients are only trustworthy if its underlying assumptions roughly hold. Four checks, done visually rather than by formula, catch most problems:

  1. Linearity. Does a straight line actually describe the relationship, or does a residuals-vs-fitted plot show a curve?
  2. Constant variance (homoscedasticity). Does the spread of residuals stay roughly constant across the range of fitted values, or does it fan out (a classic sign that a transformation, e.g. log(), might help)? Picture it: a healthy residual plot looks like a flat, even band of static across the whole width of the chart. A problem plot fans out like a megaphone — tightly bunched on one side, widely scattered on the other.
  3. Independence. Are observations genuinely independent of each other, or — as with 34 repeated annual measurements at the same five regions — correlated over time or space? A warmer-than-average year in Bayern is more likely to also be a warmer-than-average year in neighbouring Baden-Württemberg, which is a real limitation worth naming in any report that uses this model, not a reason to throw it out.
  4. Normally distributed residuals (mainly matters for small samples and for the validity of exact p-values, less for the point estimates themselves).

Exercise 4.4 — Check before you trust

plot(model_multi, which = 1)
plot(model_multi, which = 2)
Code walkthrough
  • which = 1 — residuals vs. fitted plot (checks linearity and constant spread).
  • which = 2 — normal Q-Q plot (checks whether the residuals look normally distributed).

Q7. Does the residuals-vs-fitted plot look like a random scatter around zero, or is there a visible pattern? What would a pattern suggest about the linearity assumption?

Q8. Given the repeated-measures structure of this dataset (each region sampled every year for 34 years), what is one sentence you should add to any report interpreting model_multi’s p-values?

Q9. — Putting it together. In 3–4 sentences, write a plain-language interpretation of model_multi for a non-statistician reader: which factors matter most for temperature in this dataset, how confident are you and what are this model’s limitations?

If lm(), coefficients and \(R^2\) feel like a wall of new vocabulary at once: StatQuest — Linear Regression, Clearly Explained (Josh Starmer) builds the whole idea up visually, from a scatterplot to a fitted line, before any formula.

## Interpreting Models — and Checking Their Assumptions

A regression model's coefficients are only trustworthy if its underlying assumptions
roughly hold. Four checks, done visually rather than by formula, catch most problems:

1. **Linearity.** Does a straight line actually describe the relationship, or does a
   residuals-vs-fitted plot show a curve?
2. **Constant variance (homoscedasticity).** Does the spread of residuals stay roughly
   constant across the range of fitted values, or does it fan out (a classic sign that a
   transformation, e.g. `log()`, might help)? *Picture it:* a healthy residual plot looks
   like a flat, even band of static across the whole width of the chart. A problem plot
   fans out like a megaphone — tightly bunched on one side, widely scattered on the
   other.
3. **Independence.** Are observations genuinely independent of each other, or — as with
   34 repeated annual measurements at the same five regions — correlated over time or
   space? A warmer-than-average year in Bayern is more likely to also be a
   warmer-than-average year in neighbouring Baden-Württemberg, which is a real
   limitation worth naming in any report that uses this model, not a reason to throw it
   out.
4. **Normally distributed residuals** (mainly matters for small samples and for the
   validity of exact p-values, less for the point estimates themselves).
