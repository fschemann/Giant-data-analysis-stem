A problem-based, AI-supported learning module

Author

Welcome

Module principle

You will investigate a real-world environmental challenge using a dataset from Eurostat or an Indian open-data source such as data.gov.in, the India Meteorological Department, or the Central Pollution Control Board. You may complete only the competency areas you need for your challenge, or work through all areas for a comprehensive overview of data analysis in STEM. An AI learning buddy (Acemate, for example) accompanies you throughout the process.

Required final outputs: an interactive dashboard and a short scientific report.

1. Welcome to the Module

Environmental decisions increasingly depend on data. Researchers, policymakers and communities use data to understand environmental change, compare possible solutions and make informed decisions.

However, working with data involves more than creating graphs or calculating statistical values. You also need to ask:

  • Where does the data come from?
  • Is the data reliable?
  • Which methods are appropriate?
  • What conclusions can be drawn?
  • What uncertainties and limitations remain?
  • How can the results be communicated responsibly?

In this module, you will learn to work with real environmental data and use it to investigate a relevant sustainability challenge.

To make these skills concrete rather than abstract, competency areas 1–4 all use the same worked example: real, open climate data from the Deutscher Wetterdienst (DWD), covering five German regions across 34 years (1991–2024, CC BY 4.0) so you can practice comparing meaningfully different time periods against each other. You will meet its raw, messy version in Chapter 1, clean it yourself and then reuse the tidy result in every chapter after that — so each new method (a plot, a confidence interval, a regression model) is applied to data you already understand, instead of a new unfamiliar dataset every time.

Your central goal

Use data analysis to investigate and respond to a real-world environmental challenge.

Numbers alone rarely tell the whole story. Alongside the quantitative analysis, this module also asks you to build a qualitative view: noticing where your data comes from and what it leaves out. That’s what the occasional reflection exercise you’ll meet along the way is for; Chapter 7 picks this up once you have a challenge and dataset of your own to practice it on.

The learning resources and exercises in this module will help you develop the competencies required to complete this task.

2. How This Module Works

This module is not organised as a fixed sequence of chapters that everyone must complete in the same order. It has two parts:

  • This overview page: orientation, self-assessment and selecting your Real-World Challenge and challenge dataset (Eurostat or an Indian open-data source).
  • The eight lessons that follow: competency areas you draw on as your challenge requires them.

You will begin by selecting a Real-World Challenge. You will then analyse the task and identify which data, methods and competencies you need. Your learning process will generally follow these steps:

  1. Select a Real-World Challenge.
  2. Understand and structure the problem.
  3. Identify the data and competencies you need.
  4. Complete relevant learning units and exercises.
  5. Apply your new skills to the challenge.
  6. Interpret, communicate and reflect on your findings.

You may move between the challenge and the learning resources several times. When you encounter a new question or difficulty, your AI learning buddy can help you identify the next useful learning step.

One dataset, all the way through Rubrics 1–4

Rubrics 1–4 use a common worked example — real, open annual climate data (temperature, precipitation, sunshine duration) for five German regions from the Deutscher Wetterdienst, 1991–2024, CC BY 4.0 — so that you can build the core skills on a real, well-documented dataset before applying them to your own, messier Eurostat challenge dataset. You clean it yourself in Chapter 1; the exact same clean dataset then carries through the exploration, inference and modelling chapters, so every technique you learn is demonstrated on data you’re already familiar with — including comparing an early decade (1991–2000) against a recent one (2015–2024), the same kind of period-over-period comparison your own challenge dataset may call for. See Resources → Practice dataset for the full data dictionary.

4. Choose Your Learning Path

There are two main ways to use this module.

Challenge-Based Learning Path. Complete only the competency areas you need for your selected challenge. For example, comparing greenhouse gas emissions in two different years may mainly require Understanding and Managing Data, Exploring and Visualising Data and Scientific Communication. A more complex investigation of the factors influencing emissions may additionally require Statistical Inference, Statistical Modelling and Reproducible Research. Your AI learning buddy can help you decide which areas are relevant to your task.

Full Data Analysis Learning Path. Complete all competency areas for a comprehensive overview of the complete data analysis process in STEM — from understanding and preparing data to visualisation, statistical inference, modelling, reproducibility and communication.

Important

You do not have to complete every competency area to solve your challenge. However, completing all areas will provide you with a comprehensive overview of data analysis in STEM.

5. Meet Your AI Learning Buddy

Your AI learning buddy accompanies you throughout the module as a learning navigator, not an answer generator. You can ask it for support when you:

  • do not fully understand the challenge
  • need help structuring your investigation
  • are unsure which competencies you need
  • are looking for a suitable exercise or learning resource
  • need an explanation of a statistical concept
  • encounter a problem with R code
  • want to check your understanding
  • need help interpreting a result
  • are unsure about your next learning step

What it can do: explain concepts in different ways · recommend relevant resources · guide you through intermediate steps · ask questions to check your understanding · give feedback on your reasoning · help you plan your learning path.

What it will not do: complete the challenge for you. You remain responsible for selecting appropriate methods, checking code and calculations, evaluating data quality, interpreting results and drawing scientific conclusions.

Use AI critically

Your AI learning buddy supports your scientific thinking. It does not replace it. Always check AI-generated code, explanations and interpretations critically — see Chapter 6, AI-Assisted Learning and Analysis for how to do this well.

Working effectively with your AI learning buddy is itself a competency taught in this module (Rubric 6): the module models a Socratic dialogue style of AI use, in which it asks questions back rather than handing you finished answers, so that you practice “computational offloading” of routine steps while keeping the scientific judgement with you.

No access to Acemate? Any AI learning buddy works just as well

Acemate is this module’s default recommendation for an AI learning buddy. But the module doesn’t depend on it: if your Acemate access ever lapses (e.g. a licence renewal gap) or you simply prefer a different tool, any general-purpose AI assistant can fill the same role, using the same Socratic-dialogue approach described above.

  • General chat assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, …) — paste the relevant lesson text, or your own code and question, directly into the chat.
  • NotebookLM is particularly well suited to this module, because it answers grounded in documents you give it rather than from general knowledge alone. Add this module’s pages as sources (one web-page link per chapter — NotebookLM accepts up to 50 sources per notebook, far more than the ten pages here), or, if the site isn’t hosted online, add the .qmd files directly: NotebookLM also accepts plain text and Markdown files, so it can read this module’s chapters exactly as written.

Whichever tool you use, the boundary described above (“what it should and shouldn’t do for you”) still applies — that’s a property of how you use AI, not of which AI you use.

6. Assess Your Starting Point

Before selecting your challenge, complete this short, ungraded self-assessment. Its purpose is to help you (and your AI learning buddy) identify your prior experience and likely learning needs — it does not determine whether you can participate in the module.

Self-assessment prompts (talk these through with your AI learning buddy if you like):

  1. Have you worked with R before? At what level (never / a little / comfortably)?
  2. Can you distinguish between numerical and categorical variables?
  3. Can you interpret common data visualisations (histograms, boxplots, scatterplots)?
  4. Are you familiar with confidence intervals or hypothesis tests?
  5. Have you worked with regression models?
  6. Have you used Quarto or GitHub before?
  7. Have you previously used an AI tool to support data analysis? How?

There are no right or wrong starting points. You do not need previous experience in every area. Your AI learning buddy can use your answers to recommend suitable introductory or advanced material within each chapter.

7. Select a Real-World Challenge

You will now explore the Challenge Library and select an environmental or sustainability topic that interests you. For your investigation, you will use an environmental dataset from either of two sources:

  • Eurostat, which provides comparable statistical data for European countries across a wide range of environmental topics and time periods; or
  • an Indian open-data source, most straightforwardly the Open Government Data (OGD) Platform India (data.gov.in), which publishes datasets from bodies such as the India Meteorological Department (IMD) and the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) under the Government Open Data License – India (GODL) — attribution-required and functionally comparable to CC BY, but not the same license, so cite it by its own name, not as “CC BY 4.0”.

Both options are equally valid starting points; pick whichever matches your challenge topic and interests you more. What matters is that the source is a recognised public body (not a blog, a news article’s chart, or an unsourced spreadsheet) and that its licence is clear enough to cite properly in your final report.

Explore environmental data in the Eurostat Data Browser · Explore Indian open data on data.gov.in

Possible challenge areas include:

  • climate change and greenhouse gas emissions
  • renewable energy
  • water use and water availability
  • waste generation and recycling (incl. microplastics)
  • air pollution
  • biodiversity
  • land-use change
  • environmental pressures from economic activities

For each challenge, you will need to:

  • prepare a short introduction to the environmental issue
  • draft a specific analytical task
  • find a suitable dataset (Eurostat or an Indian open-data source)
  • select possible countries, regions, sectors or years to compare
  • indicate the level of difficulty
  • choose recommended competency areas
  • create a personal learning pathway toward a dashboard and scientific report

You may select one of the suggested challenges or develop your own analytical question based on an appropriate Eurostat or Indian environmental dataset. If your interest leans toward climate change, temperature trends, or extreme-weather frequency, you will already be warmed up from the practice dataset used across Rubrics 1–4 — the same kind of region-level, period-over-period comparison you practiced there (Chapters 2–4) transfers directly, whether your own dataset ends up being Eurostat’s temperature series or IMD’s regional rainfall records. If your interest leans instead toward water, wastewater, or groundwater, Eurostat’s water-use, water-quality and water-stress indicators, or India’s Central Water Commission and CPCB data are natural starting points too.

Example Challenge: Greenhouse Gas Emissions in Europe

The European Union has committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions. However, emissions have not developed equally across all countries and economic sectors.

Your task: Compare greenhouse gas emissions in two selected years. Identify which countries or sectors contributed most to the observed changes and explain the environmental significance of your findings.

Available data: Eurostat greenhouse gas emissions data. You may, for example, compare two selected years, compare several European countries, compare economic sectors, identify the largest absolute or relative changes, or examine developments over a longer time period.

Required final output

You will present your results in an interactive dashboard, built with shinydashboard and a short scientific report. The dashboard should make the most important patterns and comparisons accessible. The report should explain your analytical question, methods, results, uncertainties and conclusions.

Your Challenge Selection Task

Before beginning your data analysis, submit a short proposal for your Real-World Challenge. This proposal forms the starting point for your further work in the module.

1. Introduce the Real-World Challenge. Briefly describe the environmental or sustainability issue you would like to investigate: what the central environmental problem is, which geographical area, countries or sectors are involved, why the issue is relevant and how data analysis can contribute to a better understanding of it. Ground this in at least one or two academic or scientific sources (a journal article, a report chapter from a body like the IPCC or UNEP, a textbook) rather than only news articles or the dataset’s own metadata, so your framing of the problem is scientifically informed. Keep this concise — its purpose is environmental context.

2. Formulate a Research Question. Develop one clear, manageable research question answerable with the selected data. It should specify the environmental indicator or phenomenon, the countries/sectors/categories and the period or years to compare. Example: “How did greenhouse gas emissions from different economic sectors change in Germany between 2005 and 2023 and which sectors contributed most to the overall reduction?” Your question may be adjusted later if initial exploration shows the required data are unavailable or the question is too broad.

3. Select and Justify a Dataset (Eurostat or an Indian source). Provide the dataset title, code (where available), a link, a short description and a justification: which variables does it contain, which countries/regions/sectors and years are included, in which units and are there visible gaps or limitations? State the licence explicitly (e.g. “Eurostat — free re-use with attribution” or “data.gov.in — GODL-India”) — this is the same provenance discipline from Chapter 1. Do not select a dataset only because it is related to your general topic — make sure its variables, geographical coverage and time frame actually match your research question.

4. Define the Temporal Scope. Choose between:

  • Minimum requirement: comparison of at least two years (e.g. a baseline year and the most recent available year, or years before/after a policy intervention).
  • Advanced option: a time-series analysis across several consecutive years, to identify long-term trends, periods of increase or decline and cross-country/sector comparisons.

Required submission (≈300–500 words): title, environmental problem (with at least one or two academic or scientific sources cited), research question, selected dataset (source, link + code/identifier, licence), justification, selected countries/regions/sectors/categories/indicators, temporal approach and why the time period is meaningful.

Your AI learning buddy can help you narrow down a broad environmental topic, formulate a clear and answerable research question, identify the data required, examine whether a dataset matches your question, compare possible Eurostat or Indian datasets and check whether your selected years are meaningful. It may ask: “What exactly do you want to compare?”, “Does the dataset contain the countries or regions, sectors and years you need?”, “Would a two-year comparison be sufficient, or would a time series give a more meaningful answer?” — but you remain responsible for the final choice and its justification.

8. Understand and Narrow Down Your Challenge

Most environmental challenges are initially broad and complex. Before beginning your analysis, turn your selected topic into a specific, manageable investigation. Your AI learning buddy can guide you through questions such as: What is the central environmental problem? What exactly are you expected to investigate? Which countries, regions, sectors or years could you compare? Which variables might be relevant? What information is available in the dataset? What do you already know and what do you still need to find out? What would a convincing answer look like?

At the end of this stage you should have: a clearly defined analytical question, a selected dataset, a comparison or analytical focus and an initial idea of the required methods.

9. Create Your Personal Learning Path

After narrowing down your challenge, create a personal learning path with support from your AI learning buddy. First, consider: which parts of the dataset do you need to understand? Do you need to clean or transform the data? Which visualisations could help investigate the question? Do you need a statistical test? Do you need to model relationships between variables? How will you document and communicate your results?

A possible learning path:

Understanding and Managing Data → Exploring and Visualising Data → Statistical Inference → Statistical Modelling → Reproducible Research → Scientific Communication

Your learning path is flexible and may change during your investigation. For example, after visualising the data, your AI learning buddy may ask: “You have identified differences between the two years. Would you now like to assess whether these differences are statistically meaningful?” You may then decide to work on Statistical Inference before returning to your challenge.

10. Complete Your Final Challenge

Your final task is to answer your analytical question using data and to document your process transparently.

Required final outputs

You will produce two connected outputs: (1) an interactive dashboard built with shinydashboard and (2) a short scientific report.

1. Interactive Dashboard. Built with shinydashboard (not a static plot or a plain Shiny app without the dashboard layout — see Chapter 8’s exact requirements), it must give a clear, accessible overview of the most important findings — not simply a display of all available data. Depending on your challenge and dataset, it should include key environmental indicators, comparisons between countries/sectors/years, appropriate charts, at least one working input control that filters what’s shown, short explanations, clear units and sources and relevant limitations. Present your findings both quantitatively and qualitatively: alongside the charts, a short written panel should say who is affected by the pattern shown and why it matters beyond this one place (see Chapter 7). Your visualisations should be scientifically accurate, clearly labelled, suitable for the data type, accessible to the intended audience and directly connected to your research question. See Chapter 8, Scientific Communication for the full, worked dashboard build with all required elements listed.

2. Short Scientific Report, with sections: Research Question, Environmental Context, Data (dataset, variables, coverage, units, limitations), Methods (cleaning, comparisons, visualisations, statistical methods/models), Results, Discussion (interpretation, uncertainty, limitations, what cannot be concluded), Conclusion and References and Data Sources.

Reproducibility. The dashboard and report should share the same transparent analytical workflow. Submit: the original data source, the code used to prepare and analyse the data, the code used to create the visualisations and dashboard and enough documentation for someone else to understand and reproduce your analysis (see Chapter 5, Reproducible Research).

The Real-World Challenge is both the central learning activity and the final demonstration of your competencies. You are not expected to use every method in the module — you are expected to select and apply the methods appropriate for your question and explain your decisions. By completing the dashboard and report, you demonstrate that you can investigate an environmental problem using real data, select and apply appropriate analytical methods, visualise findings effectively, critically evaluate evidence and uncertainty, work reproducibly and communicate scientific results to different audiences.

data.gov.in](https://www.data.gov.in/){target="_blank"}

Possible challenge areas include:

- climate change and greenhouse gas emissions
- renewable energy
- water use and water availability
- waste generation and recycling (incl. microplastics)
- air pollution
- biodiversity
- land-use change
- environmental pressures from economic activities

For each challenge, you will need to:

- prepare a short introduction to the environmental issue
- draft a specific analytical task
- find a suitable dataset (Eurostat or an Indian open-data source)
- select possible countries, regions, sectors or years to compare
- indicate the level of difficulty
- choose recommended competency areas
- create a personal learning pathway toward a dashboard and scientific report

You may select one of the suggested challenges or develop your own analytical question
based on an appropriate Eurostat or Indian environmental dataset. If your interest leans
toward climate change, temperature trends, or extreme-weather frequency, you will
already be warmed up from the practice dataset used across Rubrics 1–4 — the same kind
of region-level, period-over-period comparison you practiced there (Chapters 2–4)
transfers directly, whether your own dataset ends up being Eurostat's temperature series
or IMD's regional rainfall records. If your interest leans instead toward water,
wastewater, or groundwater, Eurostat's water-use, water-quality and water-stress
indicators, or India's Central Water Commission and CPCB data are natural starting
points too.

### Example Challenge: Greenhouse Gas Emissions in Europe

The European Union has committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions. However,
emissions have not developed equally across all countries and economic sectors.

**Your task:** Compare greenhouse gas emissions in two selected years. Identify which
countries or sectors contributed most to the observed changes and explain the
environmental significance of your findings.

**Available data:** Eurostat greenhouse gas emissions data. You may, for example,
compare two selected years, compare several European countries, compare economic
sectors, identify the largest absolute or relative changes, or examine developments
over a longer time period.

::: {.callout-important}
## Required final output
You will present your results in an interactive dashboard, built with
**[`shinydashboard`](https://rstudio.github.io/shinydashboard/)** and a short scientific
report. The dashboard should make the most important patterns and comparisons
accessible. The report should explain your analytical question, methods, results,
uncertainties and conclusions.
:::

### Your Challenge Selection Task

Before beginning your data analysis, submit a short proposal for your Real-World
Challenge. This proposal forms the starting point for your further work in the module.

::: {.exercise-box}
**1. Introduce the Real-World Challenge.** Briefly describe the environmental or
sustainability issue you would like to investigate: what the central environmental
problem is, which geographical area, countries or sectors are involved, why the issue is
relevant and how data analysis can contribute to a better understanding of it. Ground
this in at least one or two academic or scientific sources (a journal article, a
report chapter from a body like the IPCC or UNEP, a textbook) rather than only news
articles or the dataset's own metadata, so your framing of the problem is scientifically
informed. Keep this concise — its purpose is environmental context.

**2. Formulate a Research Question.** Develop one clear, manageable research question
answerable with the selected data. It should specify the environmental indicator or
phenomenon, the countries/sectors/categories and the period or years to compare. Example:
*"How did greenhouse gas emissions from different economic sectors change in Germany
between 2005 and 2023 and which sectors contributed most to the overall reduction?"*
Your question may be adjusted later if initial exploration shows the required data are
unavailable or the question is too broad.

**3. Select and Justify a Dataset (Eurostat or an Indian source).** Provide the
dataset title, code (where available), a link, a short description and a
justification: which variables does it contain, which countries/regions/sectors and
years are included, in which units and are there visible gaps or limitations? State
the licence explicitly (e.g. "Eurostat — free re-use with attribution" or "data.gov.in —
GODL-India") — this is the same provenance discipline from [Chapter
1](01-understanding-data.qmd). Do not select a dataset only because it is related to
your general topic — make sure its variables, geographical coverage and time frame
actually match your research question.

**4. Define the Temporal Scope.** Choose between:

- *Minimum requirement:* comparison of at least two years (e.g. a baseline year and the
  most recent available year, or years before/after a policy intervention).
- *Advanced option:* a time-series analysis across several consecutive years, to
  identify long-term trends, periods of increase or decline and cross-country/sector
  comparisons.

**Required submission** (≈300–500 words): title, environmental problem (with at least
one or two academic or scientific sources cited), research question, selected dataset
(source, link + code/identifier, licence), justification, selected
countries/regions/sectors/categories/indicators, temporal approach and why the time
period is meaningful.
:::

::: {.acemate-box}
Your AI learning buddy can help you narrow down a broad environmental topic, formulate a
clear and answerable research question, identify the data required, examine whether a
dataset matches your question, compare possible Eurostat or Indian datasets and check
whether your selected years are meaningful. It may ask: *"What exactly do
you want to compare?"*, *"Does the dataset contain the countries or regions, sectors and
years you need?"*, *"Would a two-year comparison be sufficient, or would a time series
give a more meaningful answer?"* — but you remain responsible for the final choice and
its justification.
:::

## 8. Understand and Narrow Down Your Challenge

Most environmental challenges are initially broad and complex. Before beginning your
analysis, turn your selected topic into a specific, manageable investigation. Your AI
learning buddy can guide you through questions such as: What is the central environmental problem? What
exactly are you expected to investigate? Which countries, regions, sectors or years
could you compare? Which variables might be relevant? What information is available in
the dataset? What do you already know and what do you still need to find out? What
would a convincing answer look like?

At the end of this stage you should have: a clearly defined analytical question, a
selected dataset, a comparison or analytical focus and an initial idea of the required
methods.

## 9. Create Your Personal Learning Path

After narrowing down your challenge, create a personal learning path with support from
your AI learning buddy. First, consider: which parts of the dataset do you need to understand? Do you
need to clean or transform the data? Which visualisations could help investigate the
question? Do you need a statistical test? Do you need to model relationships between
variables? How will you document and communicate your results?

A possible learning path:

::: {layout-ncol="1"}
Understanding and Managing Data → Exploring and Visualising Data → Statistical Inference
→ Statistical Modelling → Reproducible Research → Scientific Communication
:::

Your learning path is flexible and may change during your investigation. For example,
after visualising the data, your AI learning buddy may ask: *"You have identified
differences between the two years. Would you now like to assess whether these
differences are statistically meaningful?"* You may then decide to work on Statistical
Inference before returning to your challenge.

## 10. Complete Your Final Challenge

Your final task is to answer your analytical question using data and to document your
process transparently.

::: {.callout-important}
## Required final outputs
You will produce two connected outputs: (1) an interactive dashboard built with
[`shinydashboard`](https://rstudio.github.io/shinydashboard/) and (2) a short scientific
report.
:::

**1. Interactive Dashboard.** Built with **`shinydashboard`** (not a static plot or a
plain Shiny app without the dashboard layout — see
[Chapter 8's exact requirements](08-scientific-communication.qmd#building-the-interactive-dashboard)),
it must give a clear, accessible overview of the most important findings — not simply a
display of all available data. Depending on your challenge and dataset, it should
include key environmental indicators, comparisons between countries/sectors/years,
appropriate charts, at least one working input control that filters what's shown, short
explanations, clear units and sources and relevant limitations. Present your findings
**both quantitatively and qualitatively**: alongside the charts, a short written panel
should say who is affected by the pattern shown and why it matters beyond this one place
(see [Chapter 7](07-sustainability-problem-solving.qmd#why-global-matters-data-perspective-and-responsibility)).
Your visualisations should be scientifically accurate, clearly labelled, suitable for the
data type, accessible to the intended audience and directly connected to your research
question. See [Chapter 8, Scientific Communication](08-scientific-communication.qmd) for
the full, worked dashboard build with all required elements listed.

**2. Short Scientific Report**, with sections: *Research Question*, *Environmental
Context*, *Data* (dataset, variables, coverage, units, limitations), *Methods* (cleaning,
comparisons, visualisations, statistical methods/models), *Results*, *Discussion*
(interpretation, uncertainty, limitations, what cannot be concluded), *Conclusion* and
*References and Data Sources*.

**Reproducibility.** The dashboard and report should share the same transparent
analytical workflow. Submit: the original data source, the code used to prepare and
analyse the data, the code used to create the visualisations and dashboard and enough
documentation for someone else to understand and reproduce your analysis (see
[Chapter 5, Reproducible Research](05-reproducible-research.qmd)).

The Real-World Challenge is both the central learning activity and the final
demonstration of your competencies. You are not expected to use every method in the
module — you are expected to select and apply the methods appropriate for your question
and explain your decisions. By completing the dashboard and report, you demonstrate that
you can investigate an environmental problem using real data, select and apply
appropriate analytical methods, visualise findings effectively, critically evaluate
evidence and uncertainty, work reproducibly and communicate scientific results to
different audiences.
