6  AI-Assisted Learning and Analysis

Guiding question: How can AI support my scientific workflow without replacing my own reasoning?

In this area you will learn how to: work effectively with your AI learning buddy, formulate useful prompts, use AI to support coding and analysis, verify AI-generated suggestions and reflect critically on the role of AI in scientific work.

6.1 AI as a Learning Navigator, Not an Answer Machine

This module places AI at the centre of its “digital literacy” goal, not as a shortcut around learning but as practice for a skill you will use throughout your career: working productively with an AI system that does not simply hand you finished answers. Recall from the welcome page: your AI learning buddy structures problems, identifies competencies, recommends exercises, answers comprehension questions and supports interpretation and reflection. It does not select your methods, check your code for you, evaluate your data quality, interpret your results, or draw your scientific conclusions. Those stay yours.

6.2 Socratic Dialogue and Computational Offloading

This module’s AI approach is built on two linked ideas:

  • Socratic dialogue. Rather than asking your AI learning buddy “what’s the answer?”, you engage it in a back-and-forth: you propose an approach, it asks a clarifying or challenging question, you refine your thinking and so on. This mirrors how a good human tutor works: the value is in the questions, not just the final statement.
  • Computational offloading. You can legitimately hand off mechanical work to AI — boilerplate code, syntax lookups, first-draft phrasing — so you can spend your own cognitive effort on the parts that require judgement: which method fits the question, whether a result makes scientific sense, what a finding actually means.

Analogy: a calculator offloads arithmetic so you can focus on which calculation to run. AI can offload writing a ggplot2 boilerplate or a dplyr pipeline skeleton, so you can focus on which plot answers your research question and whether the numbers it produces make physical sense.

The distinction matters because the two are easy to blur: offloading the typing of a regression call is fine; offloading the decision of which predictors belong in your model, or what your R² means for your specific climate-data question, defeats the purpose of the module.

6.3 Prompt Literacy

A vague prompt gets a vague, generic answer. Prompt literacy is the skill of asking in a way that gets you a useful, checkable response — for coding and for conceptual questions alike.

Weak prompt Stronger prompt
“Fix my code” “This ggplot2 call throws object 'Sunshine_hours' not found. Here is my code and str(climate) output — what’s the mismatch?”
“Explain regression” “I have a multiple regression with Sunshine_hours, Year and Region predicting Temperature_C. Explain what the Region coefficients mean, using my variable names.”
“Is my p-value good?” “My hypothesis test gives p < 0.001 comparing temperature in 1991–2000 vs. 2015–2024 at n = 50 observations per group. Walk me through, step by step, what conclusions I can and cannot draw — including effect size.”

Notice the pattern: the stronger prompts give context (your actual variables, your actual numbers, your actual error message) and ask for reasoning, not just a verdict.

If you paste a lesson’s R code into your learning buddy and it “fixes” a line you didn’t ask about, don’t just accept the change — ask it to explain why the original line was wrong. If its explanation doesn’t hold up against what you learned in the chapter, the original line was probably fine.

Exercise 6.3 — Rewrite three weak prompts

Take three prompts you have actually typed into an AI tool during this module (or would be tempted to). For each, rewrite it following the pattern above: add concrete context and ask for the reasoning rather than only the answer.

Q1. Which of your three rewrites do you expect would produce the most different (and more useful) response from the original? Why?

6.4 Verifying AI-Generated Code and Explanations

AI-generated code and explanations can be wrong in ways that are easy to miss, because they are typically fluent and confident-sounding regardless of correctness. A working verification habit, for every substantial AI suggestion you use in this module:

  1. Run it, don’t just read it. Does the code actually execute against your real data, or only against an imagined version of it?
  2. Check the output against what you already know. Does a claimed “mean annual temperature of 45°C for Bayern” pass a basic sanity check against the ranges you saw in Chapter 2?
  3. Ask “why”, not just “is this right”. A one-word confirmation from an AI tool is not verification. Ask it to explain why a line of code works and check that explanation against what you independently know.
  4. Cross-check statistical claims against the module content. If an AI explanation of a p-value sounds like a yes/no verdict rather than the effect-size-plus-sample-size picture from Chapter 3, that’s a red flag, not a fact to copy into your report.

Exercise 6.4 — Spot the AI mistake

Below is a plausible-sounding but flawed AI explanation. Find the error(s) and correct them using what you’ve learned in this module.

“I ran lm(Temperature_C ~ Sunshine_hours, data = states) and got R² = 0.08 with p < 0.001, so sunshine duration clearly causes higher temperature in a strong, practically important way. Since p < 0.05, we can conclude the relationship is real and important.”

Q2. List every claim in this explanation that overreaches and explain — in your own words, referencing the relevant earlier chapter — what would need to be added or qualified for the statement to be defensible.

6.5 Reflect on Your Own AI Use

Exercise 6.5 — A short reflection (for your final report’s Methods section)

Answer briefly, for inclusion in your final scientific report:

  • Where in your challenge did you use AI for computational offloading (mechanical, low-judgement tasks)?
  • Where did you deliberately keep a decision to yourself, even though AI could have suggested an answer? Why?
  • Name one instance where you checked (and, ideally, caught) an AI suggestion that was wrong or only partially right.
