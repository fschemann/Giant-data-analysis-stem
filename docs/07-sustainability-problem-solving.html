7  Sustainability Problem Solving

Guiding question: How can data contribute to understanding and addressing this sustainability challenge?

In this area you will learn how to: translate an environmental problem into an analytical question, use data as evidence, consider different possible explanations and develop an evidence-based response.

This chapter is different from Chapters 1–6: it is not primarily a set of techniques, but a way of thinking that connects them all back to why you’re doing this module in the first place — this module’s broader aim of environmental stewardship, taking responsibility for global challenges and actively contributing to solving them, not only analysing them from a distance.

7.1 Why “Global” Matters: Data, Perspective and Responsibility

Data never arrives from nowhere. Someone decided what to measure, who would measure it and which categories a value could fall into: decisions shaped by the priorities, language and institutions of wherever that data comes from. Working with a dataset from a context other than your own is a chance to notice your own frame of reference, the assumptions you bring to a number without realising you brought them (Mezirow, 1997). If a figure surprises you, or a category doesn’t map neatly onto what you expected, that moment of friction is worth sitting with rather than smoothing over. It is usually where real learning starts.

This module’s idea of environmental stewardship follows Global Citizenship Education’s emancipatory strand (Rieckmann, 2021): environmental problems and the data describing them are shared across borders rather than isolated to wherever they happen to be measured. A warming trend measured in one place and a shifting rainfall pattern measured somewhere else are two local readings of one connected global system. Practising that recognition is itself part of this competency area, alongside the technical skill of turning a broad problem into a data-answerable question.

7.2 From a Wicked Problem to an Analytical Question

Real sustainability problems — shifting regional climates, urban heat stress, extreme weather becoming more frequent, biodiversity loss — start out broad, contested and not directly “answerable” with a dataset. The core move of this competency area is narrowing a broad problem into one specific, data-answerable question, without losing what made the problem matter in the first place.

Consider this example problem statement:

“Are the monitored weather stations showing signs of a shifting climate?”

This is not yet an analytical question — no single dataset or statistic settles it. A possible learning path, mirroring how your learning buddy would walk through it with you:

Your learning buddy asks: Which data would be relevant? Which variables do you need? How would you initially examine the data? → points you to Rubric 1, Understanding and Managing Data

Students work on: understanding variables, getting to know the dataset, checking data quality.

Your learning buddy, back at the problem: “Now that you understand the dataset, how could you explore possible patterns?” → Rubric 2, Exploring and Visualising Data

Your learning buddy: “Can you justify whether the 1991–2000-vs-2015–2024 difference you saw is meaningful, or could it just be random variation?” → Rubric 3, Statistical Inference

Your learning buddy: “Can you model the relationship between temperature and other climate variables and how much of the pattern each one explains?” → Rubric 4, Statistical Modelling

Your learning buddy, at the end: “Document your analysis and make it reproducible.” → Rubric 5, Reproducible Research

Notice what happened: the broad, contested problem (“is this safe?”) got narrowed into a sequence of specific, data-answerable sub-questions, each pointing to a competency area — without the original stewardship question disappearing. You return to it explicitly at the end (§7.4).

Exercise 7.1 — Narrow your own challenge

Take the Real-World Challenge you selected in the overview. Write down: (a) the broad problem in one sentence, (b) the specific, data-answerable research question you formulated and (c) one thing your narrower question necessarily leaves out of scope. Being explicit about (c) is not a weakness in your work — it is exactly the kind of limitation a strong Discussion section names openly.

7.3 Data as Evidence, Not Proof

A dataset provides evidence toward answering a sustainability question — it does not provide proof in the way a mathematical theorem does. Two examples worth sitting with:

  • Finding that mean temperature across five German regions is statistically higher in 2015–2024 than in 1991–2000 (Chapter 3) is evidence consistent with a real warming trend — but it is not, by itself, proof of a cause and 34 years of data from five regions of one country cannot establish a global climate mechanism on their own.
  • A GHG-emissions dataset showing “higher share of renewable energy correlates with lower emissions” across countries is evidence for the plausibility of the claim — but correlation across countries conflates many differences (industrial structure, wealth, policy history) that a single scatterplot cannot separate out.

This is why Chapters 3 and 4 exist: statistical inference and modelling are the tools that let you say, carefully, how much evidential weight a pattern actually carries.

7.4 Considering Alternative Explanations

Before treating a pattern as the explanation, deliberately generate at least one competing explanation and check whether your data can distinguish between them.

Exercise 7.3 — Generate competing explanations

Example: “Does a higher share of renewable energy correlate with lower greenhouse gas emissions?” (the example challenge from an early rubric proposal for this module).

Q1. List at least two explanations for an observed negative correlation between renewable-energy share and emissions, other than “renewables directly cause lower emissions” (for instance: wealthier countries can afford both more renewables and stricter industrial regulation; the causal arrow could run the other way if emissions targets drove renewable investment).

Q2. For your own challenge question, do the same: name one alternative explanation for the pattern you expect (or found) in your data and state whether your current dataset lets you rule it out, partially address it, or not address it at all.

7.5 From Evidence to an Evidence-Based Response

The final step connects your analysis back to the sustainability problem you started from and to who might act on it. A useful check: could a specific, named audience (a regional climate-adaptation office, an EU policy team, a citizen-science group) actually do something differently based on your conclusion, stated as you’ve written it?

Exercise 7.4 — Write your evidence-based response

In 3–5 sentences, connect your Exercise 7.1 findings back to the original broad problem: what does your evidence support, what does it leave open and who could act on it and how? This paragraph is close to a first draft of your final report’s Conclusion section (see Chapter 8).

7.6 A Short Self-Check: Global Citizenship Competencies

Goodale, Gilmore and Griffiths (2024) describe six observable competencies that environmental stewardship draws on. You don’t need to master all six to complete this module, but pausing to check where you already stand and where this challenge pushed you, is itself useful material for your final report’s Discussion section.

Rate yourself briefly, a sentence each is enough, on:

  1. Intergroup empathy. Can you describe how this environmental issue is experienced differently in a country or community other than your own?
  2. Valuing diversity. Did your analysis consider more than one region’s, country’s or group’s perspective?
  3. Social justice orientation. Did you consider who is most affected by this problem and whether that differs from who has the most power to address it?
  4. Willingness to sustain the environment. Has working through this challenge changed anything about your own everyday choices, even a small one?
  5. Intergroup helping. Does your Exercise 7.4 response name who could act on it, beyond “someone should do something”?
  6. Responsibility to act. Is there one concrete next step you personally could take or advocate for, based on what you found?

If “narrowing a wicked problem into a data question” feels abstract with no example to anchor it: Our World in Data shows, across hundreds of real topics, how broad global challenges get turned into specific, chart-able questions — browse a few entries close to your own challenge topic before starting Exercise 7.1.

If you want the deeper conceptual framing behind treating sustainability as interconnected systems rather than isolated problems: Kate Raworth’s Doughnut Economics (2017) is the widely-cited starting point and pairs well with this module’s stewardship framing. For the transformative-learning and Global Citizenship Education ideas in this chapter’s opening section, Mezirow (1997) and Rieckmann (2021) are the primary sources; Lotz-Sisitka, Wals, Kronlid and McGarry (2015) go further into transgressive social learning for anyone writing more deeply on this in their final report.

7.7 Check your understanding

  • Can you narrow a broad sustainability problem into a specific, data-answerable question while keeping the original problem visible in your final conclusion?
  • Can you name, for your own challenge, at least one alternative explanation for the pattern you found?
  • Can you state clearly what your evidence does and does not establish?
  • Can you name one assumption your own frame of reference brought to your dataset, especially if it comes from a context other than your own?
